The midterm tide

In 2026 Republicans lost the House and the Senate. Two years into a presidency, the president’s party almost always loses ground: in 45 of the 57 midterms since 1790 that can be scored. This is how 2026 compares.

What happened

The HouseRepublicans lost the House

They went in with 220 seats, two more than a majority needs. By Sunday evening the AP had called 252 seats for Democrats and 161 for Republicans, with 22 still uncalled. NBC News estimates the final count at 259 to 176: a Republican loss of 44, against 41 in Trump’s first midterm. Until the count is final, the 2026 figures on this page use that estimate.

Each bar is one midterm since 1790: the House seats the president’s party gained or lost. Below the line, it lost.

The deepest bar is 1894. Grover Cleveland, the only other president to serve two terms that were not consecutive, lost 125 seats in his second midterm, in a depression. Measured as a share of the House, these were the biggest waves.

The SenateNearly every Senate race moved toward the Democrats

Take each state’s vote for president in 2024 and set its Senate race this year beside it. In 30 of the 31 races with both a Democrat and a Republican on the ballot, the Senate vote was more Democratic. The typical race moved 18 points. Texas, which Trump won by 14, elected a Democrat by 12. The exception was Maine, where Susan Collins, who had long run ahead of her party, did about a point better than Trump and still lost.

That swing took seats Republicans had counted on. Democrats needed four. They won Maine and North Carolina, then five in states Trump carried by more than 10 points: Ohio, Iowa, Texas, Kansas and South Carolina. In the five big waves from 1994 to 2018, that happened three times in all.

No midterm since at least 1978 came as close to moving every race one way. The next were 1982 and 1986, when 29 of 33 and 29 of 34 races moved against Ronald Reagan’s party.

The statesEach wave lands somewhere else

Pick a midterm to see where the president’s party gained and lost House seats. In 1994 Democrats lost six in Washington alone, the Speaker’s among them. In 2010 they lost five each in New York, Ohio and Pennsylvania. In 2018 Republicans lost seven in California.

TurnoutAlmost as many voters as 2018

An estimated 49.1% of eligible voters cast a ballot, by the UF Election Lab’s latest count. Among midterms since 1914, only 2018 drew more, at 50.0%. Midterms always draw fewer voters than the presidential elections around them.

Some states keep far more of their presidential-year voters than others. In 2026 Maine kept 88% of its 2024 turnout and West Virginia 60%. From 2002 to 2022 the average state kept 73%. The five states that elect their governors in odd years kept 66%.

Before the vote

Did the record call it?

Before the count, the history of the midterm gave seven signals about 2026. Four held, one missed and two are still being counted.

  1. Approval and the majorityHeld

  2. The marginHeld

    Three of the four parties that went into a midterm four seats or fewer over a majority lost the House.

Three ideas that didn’t decide it

Coattails
The seats a president’s party gains in his election say almost nothing about what it loses two years later. Clinton in 1992 and Trump in 2016 lost House ground as they won, and still lost 54 and 41 seats in their midterms.
A surge in turnout
The states where turnout rose most did not swing harder against the president, in 1994, 2006, 2010, 2014 or 2018. Nor in 2026, in the Senate.
A shrinking battlefield
The number of House seats won by less than 10 points rises with each wave and falls after it, from 22 in 2004 to 89 in 2018. It has not been shrinking.

The record

What the signals rest on

The history behind each signal: every midterm since 1790 where the data reach that far, and every House race, state by state, since 1976.

1 · Approval and the majorityUnpopular presidents with thin majorities lose the House

Every president below 50% in Gallup’s last poll before a midterm has lost House seats, from Biden’s 9 in 2022 to Obama’s 63 in 2010. Whether his party also lost the House turned on how many seats it held over a majority.

Since 1946, nine presidents went in below 50% while their party ran the House. The six with 40 seats or fewer over a majority all lost it. The three with 45 or more kept it. Trump went into 2026 at 33%, with 2 over a majority.

2 · The marginSmall majorities rarely survive a midterm

Since 1830 the president’s party has lost fewer than three House seats in five of 48 midterms. Four parties went in with four seats or fewer over a majority, and three lost the House. The one that held, in 2002, had 67% approval a year after September 11.

3 · The Senate mapThe Senate follows the map more than the mood

Only a third of the Senate is up each time, and which third matters. In 1970, 1982 and 2018 the president’s party lost House seats and still gained in the Senate, because it was defending only 7, 12 and 9 seats. When it defended 20 or more, it lost Senate seats in eight of nine midterms.

Since 2006 it has lost 13 of the 14 seats it defended in states the president had lost. In states he carried by more than 10 points it seldom lost any: three in the five big waves from 1994 to 2018. In 2026 Republicans defended 22 seats and lost five in such states.

4 · The calendarSince 1952 the House has changed hands only in midterms

The last change of control in a presidential year was 1952. The changes since came in midterms: 1954, 1994, 2006, 2010, 2018, 2022 and now 2026.

5 · The membersThe newest members fall first

In 2010, 22 of the 38 Democrats first elected in 2008 who ran again lost, 58%, against 16% of those elected before them. In 9 of the 11 midterms since 1982, the president’s first-term members lost at a higher rate than the rest of his party. Republicans elected 35 new members in 2024.

Waves take the senior too: the Speaker, Tom Foley, with Dan Rostenkowski and Jack Brooks in 1994; Jim Oberstar, Ike Skelton and John Spratt in 2010; Pete Sessions in 2018.

6 · The ballotWaves show first in who runs

In the four big waves since 1976, the party out of the White House left 17 House seats or fewer uncontested. In the quiet midterms of 1990, 1998, 2002 and 2014 it left 36 to 45. In 2026 Democrats left one.

Over 12 midterms this count has tracked the president’s losses more closely than his approval has. It shows what candidates expected: strong challengers run when they think they can win.

7 · One tideIn a big wave, nearly every district moves together

Take the districts that kept their lines, with both parties on the ballot both times. In 2010, 332 of 358 moved against the Democrats. In 1994, 2006, 2014 and 2018, at least four in five moved against the president’s party. In 1990 and 1998 about half did, and the House barely changed.

The states that swing hardest are different each time.

8 · The SouthTwo midterms remade the South

In 1976 Democrats held 81 of the 108 House seats in the eleven states of the old Confederacy, 75%. By 2024 they held 43 of 142, 30%. Two midterms under Democratic presidents did two thirds of that: 1994, from 62% to 49%, and 2010, from 45% to 28%.

9 · One ballotVoters stopped splitting their tickets

On the same day, in the same state, the Senate race and the House races used to go different ways. In 1994, 16 of the 34 states with a Senate race elected a senator from the party that lost the state’s House vote. In 2014 two did.

The gap between the president’s party’s Senate and House vote in the same state averaged 8 to 13 points in every midterm from 1978 to 2006. By 2022 it was 3, when four states split, all for Democratic senators: Arizona, Georgia, Nevada and Pennsylvania.
