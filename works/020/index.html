A language nobody speaks

Emewaneme/emewaneme/

The name means “the word of the mountain”.

verb in the middle built with small words 11 consonants, 6 vowels written left to right No. 15, one of 99,999

This page made up a language when it opened: its sounds, its writing, its words and its grammar. Nobody speaks it. Every part of it is something a real language does, and the page says which. Each language has a number that brings it back.

One sentence

One English sentence, said in this language. Change the sentence, and whatever changes in the language is written in red.

në putonisema ti pano numuma pinuna
earlier fisherman DEF see two bird The fisherman saw two birds.

në putonisema ti pano su numuma pinuna
earlier fisherman DEF see NEG two bird The fisherman did not see two birds.

në putonisema ti pano numuma pinuna ta
earlier fisherman DEF see two bird Q Did the fisherman see two birds?

Who comes first, then the verb, then what, as in English, Mandarin and Swahili. “Not” is su, a word after the verb, as in German (nicht) and Dutch (niet). A question ends with ta, as Japanese ends with ka and Mandarin with ma.

A short text

The same four sentences in every language, so two can be read side by side.

në wewi rimo putonisema pano numuma neme pinuna
earlier one old fisherman see two small bird An old fisherman saw two small birds.

në pinuna nu ti awoka su

The grammar

Everything the sentence above can show, in one place.

Order
Who comes first, then the verb, then what, as in English, Mandarin and Swahili.
Who did what
Nothing marks who did what to whom except the order of the words, as in Mandarin and Indonesian.
Who receives
Whoever receives is preceded by të: “to” comes before its noun, as in English.
How many
A separate word, nu, says there is more than one, as Tagalog does with mga. After a number the noun stays as it is, “two bird”, as in Turkish and Hungarian.
The and a
“The” is ti, a word after the noun, as in Wolof and Yoruba. “A” is the word for “one”, as in German and French.
Who, on the verb
The verb is the same whoever does it, as in Mandarin, Japanese and Swedish.
When
The verb has no tenses. A word for “earlier” (në) or “later” (so) says when, as in Mandarin and Indonesian.
Not
“Not” is su, a word after the verb, as in German (nicht) and Dutch (niet).
Asking
A question ends with ta, as Japanese ends with ka and Mandarin with ma.
Describing
A describing word comes before its noun, as in English and Turkish.

What is real here

None of these languages exists. Each is drawn from a number between 1 and 99,999: the number picks the sounds, the sounds make the words, and a few hundred choices decide the grammar and the writing. Nothing is stored, and the same number always gives the same language.

The choices are real. Putting the verb last, marking the doer instead of the thing done, having no word for “the”, saying a noun twice to mean many, counting by fives, writing in columns: each is something languages do, and each note on this page names a few that do it. The common choices come up more often here, as they do among the world’s languages, so about four in five of these put the verb last or in the middle.

The seven kinds of writing are real kinds too: letters cut in straight strokes, letters hung from a line, letters joined and told apart by dots, a shape turned to change its vowel, a square for each syllable. The shapes themselves are made up, and a pen with a real nib’s geometry writes them.

The voice is made, not recorded. A soft buzz and a hiss pass through four resonances that glide from sound to sound as a mouth would, which is how formant synthesizers have worked since the 1980s. It can say any word a language here can make, and each language has a speaker of its own.

Glosses use the linguists’ abbreviations, spelled out under each sentence. Spellings in Latin letters follow one rule per sound. The examples from real languages are simplified, as examples in one line have to be.
